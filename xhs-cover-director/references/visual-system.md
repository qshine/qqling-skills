# Visual system

Choose one system according to the article's emotional job. These are art-direction grammars, not fixed templates.

## Black-yellow warning

Best for mistakes, risks, contrarian claims, failures, and tool warnings.

- near-black ground with warm off-white and safety yellow;
- large compressed headline blocks and torn edges;
- sparse warning marks, arrows, circles, and impact lines;
- highest density around the headline and focal subject;
- do not let the warning aesthetic overstate a mild claim.

## Warm personal IP

Best for experience, opinion, professional knowledge, and reflective advice.

- warm brown, cream, charcoal, and muted yellow;
- photographic or room-like depth with handwritten annotations;
- calmer hierarchy and fewer decorative fragments;
- credible, conversational energy rather than exaggerated influencer polish.

## Beige scrapbook

Best for daily observations, tutorials, gentle knowledge, and growth notes.

- kraft beige, warm white, mustard, and ink black;
- notebook paper, masking tape, rough cutouts, grids, and modest doodles;
- tactile imperfections with disciplined alignment underneath;
- avoid making every open area look like stationery.

## Hand-drawn editorial

Best for illustrated poster series, explainers, personal stories, tools, and practical knowledge. Make the drawing clarify the claim rather than merely decorate it.

Choose one grammar for the whole deliverable:

- **Marker notebook:** tools and tutorials; off-white paper, ink-black marker lines, one bright accent, diagrams, checkboxes, and simple object sketches.
- **Warm storybook linework:** personal experience and reflective advice; warm cream ground, brown or charcoal linework, restrained watercolor fills, and gentle scene depth.
- **Bold comic sketch:** mistakes, risks, and contrarian claims; heavy uneven outlines, simplified panels, impact marks, and a high-contrast accent.

Keep forms intentionally drawn but structurally credible. Use consistent stroke weight, palette, paper texture, shadow treatment, and one recurring motif. Do not imitate a named living illustrator or a recognizable creator signature.

For a series, vary the focal scene or metaphor on every page while keeping that shared grammar. Reserve blank cards, speech bubbles, or quiet paper zones for deterministic copy; never generate pseudo-handwriting.

## Shared hierarchy

1. Main headline or portrait-plus-headline relationship.
2. One evidence cue, explanatory object, or short supporting label.
3. Small doodles and texture.

Use thick white portrait outlines only when they improve separation. Keep texture behind readable copy quiet. Prefer two dominant colors plus one accent. At thumbnail size, the viewer should understand the subject and the headline before noticing decoration.

Across a poster series, lock the two dominant colors, accent, texture, and recurring motif before generating. Repeated visual grammar should create continuity; duplicated backgrounds should not substitute for page-specific art direction.
